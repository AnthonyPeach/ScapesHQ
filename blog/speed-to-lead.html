Speed to Lead: What Happens After 30 Minutes

Why the first call wins most contract jobs, what actually delays a contractor's response, and how to get first contact under two minutes.

August 28, 2026 · 6 min read

A homeowner with a wet yard fills out your form at 8:14 on a Tuesday morning. Then they fill out two more, because that's what people do — nobody submits one form and waits.

You call at 4:30 that afternoon, when the crew's loaded and you finally have a minute. By then one of the other two has already been out, walked the yard, and is sending a proposal.

You didn't lose that job on price. You lost it at 8:19.

Why the first call wins so consistently

It isn't that fast contractors are better. Three ordinary things stack up:

Nobody is waiting on you. A homeowner submitting a form has decided to solve the problem today. That decision has a short half-life. By evening the yard has dried a bit, the urgency has faded, and the whole thing moves down the list.

The first competent voice becomes the reference point. Whoever explains the problem first sets the frame — what the fix involves, what it should cost, what to watch out for. Everyone after that is compared against that explanation. Being second means arguing with a story that's already in the customer's head.

Being first often means being only. Contractors consistently underestimate how many homeowners simply stop shopping once someone shows up and seems credible. The second and third bids never get requested.

None of this requires a study to believe. Call your last ten lost leads and ask who they hired and when that contractor called back. The pattern shows up fast.

The real reasons contractors are slow

It's almost never that the owner doesn't care. It's mechanical:

  • The lead doesn't reach a person. It sits in Meta's Leads Center, or in a shared inbox nobody watches, or in an integration that quietly broke three weeks ago.
  • Nobody owns it. The owner assumes the office saw it; the office assumes the owner is handling it. Both are looking at the same unread lead.
  • The crew is in a trench. The person best qualified to call is holding a shovel, and the phone is in the truck.
  • There's no visible list. "Who needs a call right now" doesn't exist as a screen anywhere, so it exists only in somebody's memory.

Every one of these is a routing problem, not a work-ethic problem. Which is good news, because routing is fixable.

What "fast" needs to mean

Under two minutes for first contact during business hours. Under thirty minutes always, including evenings and weekends within reason.

That sounds impossible to a two-truck operation until you separate contact from conversation. First contact does not have to be a full consultation. It has to be a human acknowledgment that a real company received a real request:

"Hi Dana — this is Garrett with Ridgeline. Just got your note about standing water at the back steps. I'm on a job till 4 but I can call you properly at 4:15, or come look tomorrow morning around 9. Which works better?"

Ninety seconds. It ends the shopping.

How to actually get there

1. Route leads to a person, not an inbox. Every lead should hit a phone as a notification within a minute of submission — with the name, the address and the description already in it, so whoever picks it up can act without logging into anything.

2. Assign an owner automatically. Ambiguity is delay. Someone's name is on every lead the moment it arrives, even if it's the same name every time.

3. Make the callback list a place, not a memory. A single screen showing who came in, who's been contacted and who hasn't. If your answer to "what needs a call right now" requires scrolling a text thread, that's the problem.

4. Write the first-contact script and let anyone use it. The ninety-second version above works from a truck, a job site, or the office. It doesn't require the owner.

5. Cover evenings and weekends with honesty. A text at 8 p.m. saying you'll call at 8 a.m. beats silence until Monday, and beats a call at 8 p.m. that annoys people.

6. Monitor the pipe. If leads stop arriving, you need to know that day — not when the phone has been strangely quiet for two weeks. Ad spend keeps flowing while a broken Page connection eats leads silently.

Measure it or it drifts

Track time from lead submission to first human contact, and look at the distribution rather than the average. One lead answered in three minutes and one in nine hours averages out to something that looks acceptable and is not.

Watch the tail: what percentage took over an hour? Over a day? Those are the ones you're paying for and not working.

If you can also tie response time to outcome — which leads became site visits, which became signed jobs — you'll have the only ROI argument about lead handling that a contractor ever needs. That requires the timestamps to live on the lead record automatically, not in someone's recollection. In ScapesHQ every lead carries its intake time and its activity history, so the gap between arrival and first contact is a number you can look at rather than a feeling.

What this is worth

Do the arithmetic on your own numbers rather than trusting anyone's benchmark:

  • Take last month's leads and your close rate.
  • Split them into contacted-within-the-hour and contacted-later.
  • Compare close rates between the two groups.

Most contractors who run this the first time find a gap large enough that fixing response time is worth more than doubling ad spend — and it costs nothing per month.

FAQ

How fast is fast enough for contractor leads?

Under two minutes during business hours, under thirty always. The specific threshold matters less than being first among the two or three contractors that homeowner contacted.

What if I'm in a trench and can't call?

Send the ninety-second acknowledgment instead. A text that names the customer's actual problem and offers a specific callback time holds the lead until you can have the real conversation.

Does a fast text really beat a slower phone call?

For first contact, usually yes — it stops the shopping and buys you the real conversation later. It doesn't replace the call; it reserves your place.

Should I answer leads on weekends?

Acknowledge them, at minimum. A Saturday-morning submission that gets a reply Monday afternoon is usually gone. A short text setting a Monday call keeps it alive without wrecking your weekend.

How do I know if leads are even reaching me?

Submit a test lead through your own ad, from a phone that isn't yours, and time how long it takes to reach a person. Most contractors who do this once find something broken.

The short version

The first competent response usually wins, and the delay is almost always mechanical rather than personal. Route leads to a phone within a minute, put a name on every one automatically, keep a visible list of who needs a call, and let a ninety-second acknowledgment stand in for the full conversation. Then measure the tail, not the average.
