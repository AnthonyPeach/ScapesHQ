Downspout Drainage Done Right: Sizing, Pipe and Outfall

A 400-square-foot roof section drops roughly 250 gallons on a splash block during a one-inch rain, all of it three feet from the footing.

September 13, 2026 · 9 min read

Stand at the corner of a house during a steady rain and watch one downspout. A 2,400-square-foot roof split six ways means that single leader is draining about 400 square feet of shingle. In a one-inch rain it moves roughly 250 gallons, and the splash block puts every gallon of it into the same three feet of soil against the foundation.

Then somebody calls you about a soggy corner of lawn forty feet away, and you quote a French drain.

Sometimes the drain is the right answer. Often the roof water is most of the problem, and piping the leaders is the fix that actually changes the outcome. This is how to size it, what pipe to use, how to keep it away from the footing drain, and how to decide between a pop-up and daylight.

Roof water is a volume problem, and the volume is easy to compute

One inch of rain on one square foot is 0.623 gallons. That constant is the whole calculation. Everything else is measuring the roof and picking a design storm.

  1. Measure the plan area of the roof feeding each leader — the footprint, not the sloped surface. Pitch doesn't add water, it just moves it faster.
  2. Pick a design rainfall intensity in inches per hour. Use a short duration — 5 or 15 minutes — because that's what fills a gutter.
  3. Gallons per hour = area × intensity × 0.623. Divide by 60 for gallons per minute.
  4. Worked example: 400 sq ft × 4 in/hr × 0.623 = 997 gallons per hour, or about 16.6 gpm from that one leader.

Design intensity varies a lot by market. Pull the 10-year, 15-minute number for your county from NOAA Atlas 14 once and write it on the inside of your estimating folder. Depending on where you work, that figure commonly lands somewhere between roughly 3 and 7 inches per hour, and Gulf Coast and Florida numbers run higher than the interior West. Use your own county's number, not mine.

The point of the arithmetic isn't the pipe size. It's that you can tell a homeowner, in gallons, what's landing next to their foundation every time it rains. That number sells the work better than any adjective.

Four-inch smooth wall is plenty, and corrugated pipe is where the capacity goes

Run those gpm figures against pipe capacity and the conclusion on a residential job is almost always the same: diameter is not your constraint. The table below is full-bore Manning's flow at a 1% slope — 1/8 inch per foot — using a roughness value of about 0.011 for smooth wall and about 0.024 for corrugated. The last column converts capacity back to roof area at 4 in/hr so you can compare it to a real house.

PipeApprox. full flow at 1%Roof area served at 4 in/hrField notes
3" smooth wall~47 gpm~1,100 sq ftFine for a single leader run. Hard to snake, easy to block.
4" smooth wall (SDR-35 or SCH 40)~100 gpm~2,400 sq ftThe default. Cleanable, glues tight, holds grade.
4" corrugated, solid~46 gpm~1,100 sq ftSame hole, half the flow. Every ridge catches silt.
6" corrugated, solid~136 gpm~3,300 sq ftCapacity is fine. Snap joints separate and leak.

Treat those as ceilings, not promises. Real installs bleed capacity at every 90, at every joint that isn't glued, and anywhere the trench sagged. A single 4-inch smooth trunk taking three leaders at 16.6 gpm each is carrying about 50 gpm — half its rated capacity — which is exactly the margin you want.

What actually kills these lines:

  • Flat spots. Minimum 1%. Target 2% when the fall exists. A belly holds water, water drops silt, silt becomes a plug.
  • Hard 90s. Two 45s or a long sweep. A short-radius 90 is a place for leaves to stop.
  • Corrugated everything. The flexible stuff is faster to lay and it's the reason you get the service call in year three.
  • No cleanout. One at the top of each run, at the base of the leader, and one at any long-radius direction change. A cleanout costs you a tee and ten minutes.

Perforated pipe under a downspout waters the yard you're trying to dry

This is the single most common mistake on gutter tie-ins, and it usually comes from somebody having a roll of perforated pipe on the truck already.

A leader line is a conveyance. Its entire job is to move concentrated roof water from point A to point B without letting any of it out along the way. A French drain is a collector. Its job is to take water in through the wall of the pipe. Put roof water into a collector and you've built a machine that injects 250 gallons per storm into the soil somewhere between the house and the outfall.

Wrapping perforated pipe in fabric doesn't fix it either. The water still leaves the pipe; the fabric just decides where it silts up first. If you want the failure modes in detail, they're the same ones in the five ways French drains fail — silted fabric and a lost outfall lead the list.

Solid wall, glued, the whole way. The only intentional openings in a leader line are the inlet at the downspout, the cleanouts, and the discharge.

The footing drain and the roof leaders are two separate systems

A footing drain sits at the footing, perforated, in stone, and it relieves groundwater slowly. It's designed for a trickle over hours. Your leader line delivers 16 gpm the instant it starts raining.

Tie them together and in a real storm the roof water surcharges the footing drain, backs up into the stone envelope, and saturates the exact soil the footing drain exists to keep dry. The basement gets wetter because of the improvement. In a lot of jurisdictions the connection isn't legal in the first place — and tying leaders into a sanitary line is prohibited nearly everywhere — but the code rules vary by municipality, so check yours rather than mine.

Practical separation on a residential job:

  • Own trench where you have room. That's the clean answer.
  • Sharing a trench, shelf the leader line at least a foot above the footing drain invert and offset it horizontally. The footing drain gets the bottom of the trench and the stone; the solid line rides above it in backfill.
  • If both systems have to use the same outfall, join them outside the building footprint, downstream, where a surcharge can't travel back to the footing.
  • Never inlet the leader into a catch basin that's also the footing drain's collection point unless that basin has a gravity outlet sized for both.

Cover matters too. Ten to twelve inches over a solid line keeps it below aeration tines and shallow roots. Deep burial for frost is mostly unnecessary for a line that drains itself empty — the freezing problem lives at the discharge end, which is the next question.

A pop-up emitter buys you elevation you don't have, and costs you access

Daylight wins whenever the site gives it to you. Open end, a headwall or a stone apron, rodent screen, visible from the yard. Nothing to freeze, nothing hidden, and the homeowner can stand there in a storm and watch it run. That last part is worth more than you'd think on a service call two years later.

The test is elevation, and you measure it, you don't eyeball it. Shoot the invert at the base of the downspout and shoot the proposed discharge point. If the fall between them, divided by the run length, is under 1%, daylight isn't available on that line and neither is a pop-up — you're looking at a different design, a longer route to a lower point, or storage. When there's no outfall at all, the choice between a catch basin and a dry well comes down to a perc test and drawdown math, and roof volume is exactly the number to run it with.

Pop-ups are a real tool, not a cheat, but know what you're signing up for:

  • The last few feet hold water by design. In freezing markets that elbow ices over, and the line backs up until thaw. Set the emitter in a pocket of clean stone so weep flow leaves the box.
  • Mowers and mulch bury them. Set the lid flush, not proud, and tell the homeowner where it is — in writing, on the proposal.
  • Low flow doesn't lift the lid. A single leader in a light drizzle may never pop. That's normal; the water still leaves. Say so before they call you about it.
  • Put a cleanout tee at the last direction change upstream of the emitter. Otherwise the only access to a silted final run is a shovel.

Distance from the foundation is the other variable. Ten feet is a common minimum and a lot of markets want more on tight lots or where the grade runs back toward the house. The honest rule: the discharge has to be far enough out, and low enough, that the water it releases has somewhere to go that isn't the foundation you just piped it away from.

Price it as a line, present it as an option, and don't call it an upsell

If you diagnosed the yard properly, roof water is one of the five sources you were checking anyway — it's the second item in the one-visit wet yard diagnostic. When the roof is contributing meaningfully, gutter tie-ins aren't an add-on to the drainage job. They're part of the fix, and a yard system installed without them is a system you undersized on purpose.

Build the price from your own numbers rather than a per-leader figure off a forum:

  1. Total the trench feet. Six leaders averaging 35 feet is 210 feet, and leader runs are usually shorter and shallower than yard mains.
  2. Apply your own trench-and-pipe rate for shallow solid line — it should be lower than your French drain per-foot rate, because there's no stone and no fabric.
  3. Add materials by count, not guess: sticks of 4-inch, fittings, one downspout adapter per leader, cleanouts, emitters or headwall stone.
  4. Add restoration. Sod or seed over every foot of that trench, priced the same way you'd price it on the main system.
  5. Check the gutters themselves. If a 5-inch gutter with 2x3 leaders is already overflowing at the corner, piping the downstream end fixes nothing. Note it on the proposal as excluded work and name who should do it.

Then put it in front of the homeowner as a choice rather than a surprise. Yard system only, gutters only, or both — with the gallons-per-storm number from the first section sitting next to the gutter option so the price has something to attach to. Building the estimate with options and letting the homeowner pick is standard in ScapesHQ, and the structure of that page matters as much as the number on it; the anatomy of a proposal that gets signed the same week covers how to order the options so the middle one reads as the reasonable choice.

Most homeowners have never been told how many gallons come off their own roof. Telling them, with the arithmetic visible, does more for your close rate than any line of copy about quality workmanship.

FAQ

Can I tie downspouts into my French drain?

Not into the perforated section. A French drain is built to let water out through the pipe wall, so roof water dumped into it soaks the ground you're trying to dry. If both systems need the same outfall, run the downspouts in solid pipe and join the two lines downstream of the building, past the collection area.

What size pipe do I need for downspout drainage?

On almost every house, 4-inch smooth wall is more than enough. Multiply the roof area feeding the line by your local design rainfall in inches per hour and by 0.623 to get gallons per hour, then divide by 60 for gpm. A 4-inch smooth line at 1% slope carries roughly 100 gpm full bore, which covers around 2,400 square feet of roof at 4 inches per hour.

Should downspout drain lines be perforated or solid?

Solid, glued, for the entire run. The line's only job is to move concentrated roof water to a discharge point without releasing any of it along the way. Perforated pipe turns the line into a soaker hose between the house and the outfall, and wrapping it in fabric only changes where it plugs up first.

Do pop-up emitters clog?

They silt up when the final run has a flat spot, and they get buried under mulch or clipped by a mower. Set the emitter in clean stone so low flow can weep out, put a cleanout tee at the last direction change, and set the lid flush with grade. Also warn the homeowner that light rain often won't produce enough flow to lift the lid, which is normal.

How far from the foundation should a downspout line discharge?

Ten feet is a common minimum, and more is better on tight lots or where the grade falls back toward the house. The real requirement is that the discharge point is both far enough out and low enough that the water has somewhere to run that isn't the foundation. Local codes set their own minimums, so check what applies in your municipality.

The short version

Multiply the roof area feeding each downspout by your local design rainfall in inches per hour and by 0.623 to see the gallons landing next to the foundation every storm. Move it in solid smooth-wall pipe at 1% minimum, never perforated, never tied into the footing drain, with a cleanout at the top of every run. Daylight the end if you have the elevation; use a pop-up in clean stone with a cleanout tee if you don't. Price it as trench feet plus counted materials plus restoration, and present it as an option next to the gallons number.
