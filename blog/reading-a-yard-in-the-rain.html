Reading a Yard in the Rain: The Visit Nobody Schedules

Twenty minutes standing in a wet yard will tell you more than two hours of probing the same yard in August.

September 13, 2026 · 9 min read

The homeowner said the back yard turns into a swamp. You walked it on a dry Thursday in August. Hard ground, green grass, one soft-looking low spot about 15 feet off the back fence. You quoted 130 feet of French drain to a basin and a pumped outfall. Call it $11,400.

Then it rained. Water came off the neighbor's driveway in a sheet through a gap at the fence line and pooled exactly where you'd drawn your pipe. A perforated pipe under that much dirty surface water silts up in a couple of seasons. The right fix was a short swale and a curb along the fence, maybe $2,900 of work, and somebody else sold it three weeks later.

Nothing was wrong with your eyes. You just read the yard at the one time of year it refuses to tell the truth.

Dry ground makes you infer, moving water makes you certain

A dry-ground diagnostic is a good skill and you should have it. Silt fans, stain lines on fence pickets, moss, thinning turf, the smell of a probe sample — those are all real evidence, and there's a repeatable way to work through them in one site visit on a dry day.

But all of it is evidence water left behind. You're reading tracks, not the animal. You're reconstructing a flow path from what it deposited, which means you're guessing at direction, volume, speed and source all at once, and any one of those guesses being wrong changes the scope.

Stand in the same yard during a half-inch storm and you're not inferring anything. You see where the water enters the property. You see how wide the sheet is. You see whether that low spot fills from the sky, from the roof, from the neighbor, or from below. Twenty minutes of that closes questions that a probe never fully closes.

The readings you can only take while it's running

These are the things that exist for an hour and then disappear until the next storm.

  • Entry points. Where water crosses the property line, and how many places it does it. Off-site runoff is the source contractors most often miss, because on dry ground it looks identical to a low spot.
  • Flow width and path. A sheet 20 feet wide needs a different intercept than a concentrated channel 18 inches wide. You can measure both with your boots while it's happening.
  • Roof performance under load. Which gutters overflow, which downspouts back up at the elbow, which buried leader is surcharging and spitting at the joint. This is the fastest scope-changer there is, because roof water is cheap to fix compared to a full pipe and outfall and it's often the whole problem.
  • Surface versus seepage. Water running over a bank and water weeping out of the face of it look the same once the grass dries. In the rain they're obviously different, and they get completely different fixes.
  • Whether the existing system is taking water. A basin that's accepting flow and a basin that's burping brown water back up at you are both just a grate in August.
  • Whether the outfall is available. Your dry-day plan to daylight into the ditch is worth nothing if the ditch runs full during the same storm that floods the yard. You only learn that by looking at both at once.
  • The real footprint. Not the area that's stressed after a wet season — the area under water in this storm, which you can flag.

The window is narrower than the storm

There are three separate windows and they answer different questions.

During the rain is the only time to read entry points, flow paths and roof behavior. Everything in that first list bullet through the fourth is a during-rain observation. Fifteen minutes is usually enough.

The first hour after it stops is for extent and drawdown. Flag the edge of standing water, stick a folding rule in the deepest point, note the time and the depth. Come back in an hour, or sit in the truck and do your notes, and take a second depth. Three inches at 4:10 and two and a quarter inches at 5:10 is three quarters of an inch per hour, and that number is what sizes a dry well or tells you infiltration isn't an option at all. The math from there is in catch basin versus dry well.

Six to twenty-four hours later is the tell for subsurface water. Surface water that has somewhere to go is gone by then. Anything still holding after a day is a soil, compaction or water table issue, and no amount of grading will fix it.

Write down how much rain actually fell. A yard that floods in a 0.3-inch shower has a different problem than a yard that only floods in a two-inch event, and you can't tell them apart from photos. Use whatever gauge or radar estimate your phone gives you and record it as a note — "0.7 in over about 90 minutes" — so that in December you know which storm you were standing in. What counts as a design storm varies a lot by region, so build that judgment from your own market rather than a national figure.

The dry column isn't useless. It's how you build a scope. The wet column is how you find out the scope is aimed at the right water.

Flag it wet, shoot it dry

Shooting grades in the rain is miserable and the numbers come out sloppy. Don't try.

Instead, carry a bundle of marking flags and set them along the edge of standing water and along the flow path while it's visible. Thirty seconds per flag, no instrument. Then come back on a dry day with the laser and shoot the flags. You've now got real elevations on the actual high-water line instead of the one you estimated, which is the difference between a swale that intercepts and a swale that gets bypassed.

The rest of what to carry is short. Boots you can stand in, a folding rule for depths, flags or paint, and your phone for narrated video — say the time, the address and which direction you're facing at the start of every clip, because in four months you will not remember. A soil probe if the ground's soft enough to take one. That's it.

Narrated video matters more than photos. A still of a puddle proves a puddle. Ten seconds of you saying "that's coming through the fence from the neighbor's drive, about 15 feet wide" is an argument you can replay to the homeowner when they ask why your bid doesn't match the guy who quoted 130 feet of pipe.

How to ask for the rain visit, and what it costs you

Ask at the end of the first visit, while you're still standing in the yard. The line that works is plain: "Next time it rains hard I'd like to come stand back here for ten minutes. You don't need to be home, I won't knock, and it'll change what I quote you."

Not needing them home is the whole trick. It turns a scheduled appointment — which is what makes rain visits impossible — into a drive-by you can fit into any wet morning. Get permission once and you've got it for the season.

Run your own cost on it. Say your truck and your time run $65 per hour loaded — substitute your real number — and a drive-by is 25 minutes door to door. That's 0.42 hours × $65, about $27.

Now price the alternative. Pull your last 20 drainage jobs and count the ones where you went back out because water behaved differently than you expected. Say it's three. A return trip with two guys for a half day is roughly 8 loaded hours; at $55 per loaded hour that's $440 a trip, $1,320 across those 20 jobs, or $66 per job spread across everything you sold. If a $27 drive-by prevents even one of those three, it paid for itself several times before you count the jobs you won by quoting the smaller correct fix — which is usually the cheaper bid, and quoting the smaller fix wins work.

If a homeowner is pushing for a hard diagnosis on a complicated site, charging for the wet visit is defensible and the framing is the same as any other paid diagnostic — how to price it from your own numbers applies without modification. Most contractors will find the free drive-by easier to sell and cheap enough not to bother metering.

When you genuinely can't get there, the fallback is homeowner video, and you have to give instructions or you'll get useless footage. Tell them: stand at the back door, hold the phone steady, pan slowly left to right, say out loud what time it is and how long it's been raining. Three clips at the worst of it beats twenty photos afterward.

Building a rain list you actually work

Rain visits don't happen because nobody plans them. The fix is a standing list, not motivation.

  1. Keep four to eight open properties on a list with addresses, sorted by which ones cluster on the same side of town.
  2. Check the forecast the night before, the way you already do for crew scheduling.
  3. When rain is likely, block the first two hours of that morning as unscheduled. Don't sell them.
  4. Drive the cluster. Ten to fifteen minutes a property, video and flags, no knocking.
  5. Same day, write two or three sentences of what you saw against the property record while the storm is still in your head.

That last step is the one that decays. A rain observation is worth a lot more when it's attached to the address rather than living in a camera roll — ScapesHQ keeps properties as their own records linked to the client, so what you saw in March is still sitting there when that street calls you in October. What it won't do is tell you rain is coming: we haven't built automated playbooks or customer SMS, so the trigger stays your weather app and your own habit.

Two wet mornings a month through your rainy season is maybe eight drive-bys. That's the whole program. It costs less than one return trip and it's the closest thing to certainty this trade offers.

FAQ

How much rain do I need for a wet site visit to tell me anything?

Enough to produce runoff, which in most soils means a steady rain rather than a passing sprinkle. The more useful habit is recording how much fell — a yard that floods in a third of an inch is a different problem than one that only floods in a two-inch storm. What counts as a typical or design storm varies a lot by region, so build that sense from your own gauge readings over a season.

Do I need the homeowner to be home for a rain visit?

No, and asking for permission to come without them is what makes these visits possible. Get verbal permission at the end of your first visit, tell them you won't knock, and you can drive by any wet morning without coordinating schedules. Send them a short video afterward so they know you were there.

Should I charge for a site visit in the rain?

A ten to fifteen minute drive-by usually costs you less than $30 all in, so most contractors fold it into the bid. If the site is complicated and the homeowner wants a full diagnosis with a written finding, price it like any other paid diagnostic and credit it against the job if they sign.

What can I still learn if I get there after the rain has stopped?

Within the first hour you can flag the extent of standing water and take two depth readings an hour apart to get a drawdown rate, which is what sizes infiltration. Six to twenty-four hours later, anything still holding water points to soil, compaction or a high water table rather than surface runoff. What you lose by arriving late is entry points and flow paths, which only exist while water is moving.

Can I diagnose a yard from the homeowner's phone video?

Partly, if you tell them exactly what to shoot. Ask for three slow pans from fixed spots, with them saying the time and how long it's been raining. You'll usually get entry points and roof behavior out of it, but not depths or drawdown, so treat it as a reason to schedule your own look rather than a replacement for one.

The short version

A dry-ground diagnosis reads the tracks water left behind; a wet visit shows you the water. Ask permission on the first visit to come back unannounced during the next storm, then spend fifteen minutes recording entry points, flow width, roof performance and whether the outfall is already full — and flag the high-water edge so you can shoot real elevations on a dry day. Price it against your own numbers: a 25-minute drive-by at your loaded hourly rate is cheap next to one return trip caused by scoping the wrong water.
