The Week After You Send a Proposal: Day 1, 3 and 7

You sent a $9,400 drainage proposal Tuesday afternoon. It's Friday morning and there's nothing — no reply, no call, no signature.

September 13, 2026 · 10 min read

Most contractors treat that silence as an answer. It usually isn't. It's a homeowner who read it on their phone in a parking lot, meant to talk to their spouse about it, and then had a week.

The week after the send is the cheapest work in your business. You've already paid for the lead, the drive, the hour on site and the time building the estimate. Everything that happens now costs you a few minutes and nothing else.

The Week After the Send Is Worth More Than the Site Visit

Run your own numbers on this before you decide follow-up is a nice-to-have.

Say you send 12 proposals a month at an average of $9,400, and five of them sign. That's $47,000 booked. Now say a disciplined week of follow-up moves one more proposal a month from cold to signed. That's another $9,400 a month, and over an eight-month season, 8 × $9,400 = $75,200.

What does it cost you? Three touches per proposal, four minutes each, is 12 minutes. Twelve proposals a month is 144 minutes — 2.4 hours. If you value your own office time at $75 to $125 an hour depending on your market and what else you'd be doing, that's roughly $180 to $300 of your time a month.

$240 of your time against $9,400 of booked work. That's the trade. Substitute your own average job size and close rate — the ratio stays lopsided in almost every market.

This is a different animal from the follow-up you do on a lead that never got a proposal. That one is longer, looser and mostly about staying in front of someone who hasn't committed to anything. We covered that cadence in why leads go cold after the first call. Proposal follow-up is short, has a hard end, and ends with you asking a direct question.

Read the View Record Before You Decide What to Say

If you send a PDF attachment, you're guessing all week. You don't know if it arrived, if it opened, or if it went to spam. That's one of the quieter costs we laid out in the case against sending PDFs.

A proposal that lives at a link gives you a page-load record instead. That's real evidence, and it's better than an email open pixel — pixels fire on preview panes and image pre-fetch, and they miss entirely when images are blocked. A page load means a browser actually rendered your scope and your number.

It still doesn't tell you everything. It doesn't tell you who loaded it. A forwarded link opened by a brother-in-law who does concrete looks the same as the homeowner reading it twice. Treat view data as a hint about what to say, not proof of intent.

Here's how to read it:

  • No views by day three. This is a delivery problem, not a pricing problem. Wrong address, spam folder, or the email got buried under 40 others. Call. Read them the link over the phone if you have to.
  • One view, shortly after you sent it. It landed and didn't hold. Something in it stopped them — usually the number, sometimes a line they didn't understand.
  • Two or three views spread across days, often evenings. It's being discussed. Someone who wasn't on site is being shown it. Your job now is to make it easy for that second person to say yes.
  • Repeated views on the options page. They're comparing your own options against each other, not against another contractor. A short call that explains the difference in plain terms usually closes this one.
  • A view spike a week after you'd given up. They just got another quote and they're re-reading yours to compare. Call that day.

One honesty note, because it matters for how you set this up. ScapesHQ gives you the homeowner review page, a send history, and a permanent activity timeline on the record, so you can see what went out and when it was opened. It does not have automated playbooks or customer SMS, and those aren't coming this quarter. The reminders for day one, day three and day seven are yours to set — calendar, phone alarm, whatever you'll actually look at. The record tells you what happened; the nudging is still on you.

Day One: Confirm It Arrived and Name the Next Step

Send this the same day, within a few hours of the proposal, or first thing the next morning. It is not a check-in. It's a short message that does three jobs: confirms delivery, points at the one thing you want them to notice, and tells them what happens next.

Hi Dana — just sent the proposal for the backyard. The main thing to look at is Option B, where we tie the two downspouts into the same trunk line. It's about $700 more than Option A and it's the one I'd do on my own house, because the roof water is what's keeping that corner wet. I'll follow up Thursday if I haven't heard from you. If you want to sign before then, the button's at the bottom of the page.

Three things that message does that a generic "let me know if you have questions" doesn't:

It gives them a recommendation. Homeowners who get three options and no opinion tend to choose nothing. It names a specific dollar difference, which makes the choice concrete. And it sets the next contact yourself, so your day-three call isn't a surprise and isn't pestering — you told them it was coming.

If your proposal itself isn't doing this work already, fix the document before you fix the follow-up. The structure that makes this easy — the recommendation on page one, options that differ on scope rather than quality, ten-second signing — is laid out in the anatomy of a proposal that gets signed the same week.

Day Three: Ask the Question They Haven't Asked You

Day three is a phone call. Not an email. If they don't pick up, leave a voicemail and then text the same content so it's readable.

Homeowner hesitation on a drainage or hardscape proposal is almost always one of four things:

  1. The number is higher than they expected.
  2. They're not sure it'll actually fix the problem.
  3. They're worried about what the yard looks like when you leave.
  4. They feel like they should get another quote.

Don't wait for them to raise one. Put two of them on the table yourself: "I wanted to check whether it's the number that's giving you pause, or whether you're not sure it'll hold in a real storm. Either one's a fair question and I'd rather talk about it than have you sitting on it."

Naming the objection costs you nothing and moves the conversation past the polite stall. A homeowner who says "honestly it's more than we budgeted" has just handed you something you can work with. A homeowner who says "we're still thinking about it" has handed you nothing.

If they're getting another quote, say so out loud and tell them what to compare: pipe type, depth, whether the other bid includes restoration, and where the water actually goes. You'll lose some of these. You'll win more of them than you would by pretending the other quote doesn't exist.

Day Seven: Ask for a Decision With a Date Attached

By day seven you've confirmed delivery, offered a recommendation and surfaced the objection. Now you ask.

The ask works when it's attached to something true. Not "this price expires Friday" if it doesn't. Use your schedule, because your schedule is real:

I've got the week of October 6 open and it's the last full week before I'm booked into November. I can hold it for you through Thursday. If it's not the right time, tell me and I'll release it — no hard feelings, and I'll check back in the spring.

That gives them a real reason to decide and an honorable way to say no. Both matter. A homeowner who feels cornered goes quiet permanently; a homeowner who's given permission to decline often declines and then explains why, which is worth more to you than the silence.

Material and fuel costs move, and they don't always move down. If your proposal carries a price-validity window, put a real date on it at the time you send, and honor it exactly. A deadline you quietly extend twice teaches the homeowner that your numbers are soft.

Move Scope, Timing or Sequence Before You Move Price

When a homeowner says it's more than they budgeted, the reflex is to shave 10% off. Look at what that actually does.

Take the $9,400 job. Say your direct costs — labor, pipe, stone, fabric, spoil haul, restoration — come to $6,600. Your gross profit is $2,800, just under 30%.

Now take 10% off the price. You've given up $940. Your costs haven't changed a dollar. Profit drops from $2,800 to $1,860 — you handed over a third of your margin for a discount the homeowner perceives as modest. Run it with your own cost split and the shape is the same: a small price cut is a large profit cut.

These levers reduce the price without doing that to you:

LeverWhat you sayWhat it does to your margin
Cut scope"We seed the trench line instead of sodding it — saves about $600."Price and cost both drop. Margin percentage holds.
Phase the work"Trunk line and the two downspouts this fall, the side yard basin next spring."Full price on both phases, just spread out.
Shift timing"If you can take the week of the 20th, I've got a gap I'd rather fill."No price change. You fill an idle crew week.
Homeowner supplies labor"If you haul the spoil yourself, that's the $450 dump and trucking line off."Price drops by your actual cost. Margin untouched.
Straight discount"I can do 10% off."Comes 100% out of profit. $940 off a $2,800 margin.

The first four all require you to know what each line in your estimate costs you. If your estimate is a lump sum, you have nothing to cut except profit — which is one more argument for building estimates line by line in the first place.

Silence Past Day Fourteen Is a No — Record It and Move On

After the day-seven ask, send one close-out message around day fourteen and then stop.

Dana — I'm going to assume the timing isn't right on this one and take it off my board. The proposal link stays live if you want to come back to it. If the corner's still wet after the next big rain, call me and we'll pick it up from there.

Close-out messages get more replies than any touch except the first. Some of them are "actually, yes, sorry — send me the link again." Some are "we went with someone else." That second one is useful too, and you should ask what the other bid said.

Then write down what happened. Not in your head. On the record: the date you sent it, the dates it was viewed, the objection they gave you, and the reason it died. Thirty of those tell you things no benchmark can — that your Option B closes twice as often as Option A, or that proposals over $12,000 need a second in-person conversation before anyone signs.

Dead proposals aren't waste. They're a seasonal list. A homeowner who didn't do drainage in September is standing in the same puddle in March, and you already have their scope, their measurements and their number. Calling that list the week after the first heavy spring rain is the cheapest lead source you'll ever have.

FAQ

How many times should I follow up on a proposal before giving up?

Three active touches plus one close-out message is enough for most residential jobs. Day one confirms delivery and gives a recommendation, day three surfaces the objection by phone, day seven asks for a decision with a real schedule date attached, and around day fourteen you close it out and move the job to your seasonal list. More than that starts to read as desperate and gives the homeowner a reason to expect a discount.

Should I call or email after sending a proposal?

Both, in that order of importance. Day one works as a short email or text because it's mostly informational. Day three should be a phone call, because the objection you need to hear rarely gets typed out. If they don't answer, leave a voicemail and send the same content as a text so it's readable at a glance.

What does it mean if the homeowner never opened my proposal?

It almost always means a delivery problem rather than a rejection. Check the address you sent to, ask them to look in spam or promotions, and be ready to read them the link over the phone. A proposal that lives at a link gives you a page-load record so you can tell the difference between not interested and never received, which an emailed PDF cannot.

Is it fair to put an expiration date on a residential proposal?

Yes, as long as it's true and you honor it. Material and fuel prices move, and a 30-day validity window on pricing is normal in most markets. What damages you is setting a deadline and then quietly extending it twice, which teaches the homeowner that your numbers are negotiable and your schedule isn't real.

Should I discount to close a proposal that's gone quiet?

Not as a first move. On a $9,400 job with $6,600 in direct costs, a 10 percent discount takes $940 straight out of a $2,800 gross profit — about a third of your margin for a price cut the homeowner sees as small. Cut scope, phase the work, shift the timing to a slow week, or let the homeowner handle spoil haul instead. Those reduce price and cost together.

The short version

Follow a proposal with three touches and a close-out: day one confirms it arrived and names your recommendation with a dollar difference, day three is a phone call that puts the likely objection on the table yourself, day seven asks for a decision tied to a real schedule date, and day fourteen closes it out so the homeowner can say no honorably. Use the view record to decide what to say — no views by day three is a delivery problem, repeated evening views mean a second decision-maker is involved. When price is the objection, move scope, timing or sequence before you move the number, because a 10 percent discount on a 30 percent margin costs you a third of your profit.
