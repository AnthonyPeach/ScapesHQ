Trencher vs Mini Excavator on a Drainage Job

The gate is 36 inches, the run is 150 feet, and the machine you pick decides how much lawn you're putting back.

September 23, 2026 · 11 min read

A 36-inch side gate decides more about your margin on a residential drainage job than the pipe spec does. It tells you which machine gets in, which sets your trench width, which sets how much turf you're replacing when you leave.

Most contractors pick the machine out of habit — whatever's already on the trailer, whatever the rental yard had last time. That works right up until the habit machine is wrong for the yard, and you find out at 8:15 with three people standing in the driveway.

Here's how the three common choices actually compare on a drainage trench, and the arithmetic on a 150-foot run at 24 inches deep so you can drop your own rates in.

The three machines aren't the same job at three speeds

A walk-behind trencher is a chain on a boom with an engine behind it. It cuts a narrow slot fast in soil that wants to be cut, windrows the spoil in a neat crumb line on one side, and does nothing else. It can't dig a pit, it can't turn a corner without you backing it out, and a 3-inch root stops it cold.

A compact ride-on trencher is the same idea with more horsepower, a deeper boom, and usually a backfill blade on the front. It's two to three times faster in the cut and it puts most of the spoil back for you. It is also wider than most residential gates, and it leaves tire or track marks on both sides of the trench.

A mini excavator is slower per foot of straight trench and it digs a wider hole. In exchange it digs any depth you need, turns any corner, digs the catch basin sump and the dry well pit, sets stone and pipe, loads spoil into a buggy, pulls roots, and breaks rock if you hang a hammer on it.

The spans below are across a typical rental fleet, not one model. Pull the spec sheet for the exact unit you're reserving — boom length and retracted track width vary a lot inside a single model line.

Measure the narrowest point, not the gate

Gate width is the number everyone writes down and it's usually not the binding constraint. The binding constraint is the AC condenser pad, the gas meter, the retaining wall corner, or the two shrubs nobody is going to let you take out.

Walk the access route on the site visit with a tape and write down the tightest measurement plus where it is. "42-inch gate, 34 inches past the meter" is a usable note. "Gate is fine" is not.

Two things that catch people out. A mini excavator's published width is usually the retracted track width, and the blade may be wider than the tracks — check both. And a walk-behind trencher that measures 32 inches at the wheels still needs swing room for the operator's hands and a straight approach, so a 34-inch pinch point with a fence post on one side is tighter than it reads.

If the only way in is under 36 inches, your realistic options are a walk-behind, a 1-ton class mini with retractable tracks, or taking a fence panel out. Pulling three fence boards and putting them back is 45 minutes and it opens up the whole decision. Price it as a line item and ask on the site visit.

Trench width is a restoration number before it's a dig number

Here's the part that gets underweighted. The customer will forget what the trench looked like within a week. They will look at the lawn every day for a year.

Trench width drives three costs at once. It drives spoil volume, which drives haul-off or spreading time. It drives the width of turf you destroy. And on a wider machine, the tracks and the spoil pile sit outside the trench, so the disturbed band is far wider than the cut itself.

Run the volume. A 150-foot run at 24 inches deep:

  • 6-inch trencher: 150 × 0.5 × 2.0 = 150 cu ft ÷ 27 = 5.6 bank cubic yards
  • 8-inch ride-on: 150 × 0.67 × 2.0 = 200 cu ft ÷ 27 = 7.4 bank cubic yards
  • 12-inch bucket: 150 × 1.0 × 2.0 = 300 cu ft ÷ 27 = 11.1 bank cubic yards

Those are bank yards, in-ground. Once it's out it swells 20 to 30 percent depending on soil, so the 11.1 becomes roughly 14 loose yards sitting on the grass. If you're not already carrying swell in your bids, the swell and shrinkage math is worth an hour of your time — it's the difference between one buggy trip and three.

The mini excavator moves twice the dirt of the walk-behind on the same run. It moves it faster, which is real. But it also has to put it somewhere, and that somewhere is lawn.

The 150-foot run, priced three ways

Same job all three times: 150 feet, 24 inches deep, one straight run, sandy loam over clay, open back yard, 42-inch gate, daylight outfall. Three people on site.

Four inputs you should replace with your own before you trust any of this:

  • Loaded crew cost. Run $38–$45 per person per loaded hour in most markets, or better, work out your actual figure — the cost-per-billable-hour math gets you there. I'll use $40, so a three-person crew is $120/hr.
  • Rental day rates. From your yard's sheet, today. I'll use $180 walk-behind, $500 ride-on, $320 for a 2-ton mini.
  • Delivery. Round trip, $75–$150 each way in most markets, often free inside a radius. The walk-behind rides on your own trailer, so $0.
  • Restoration. Seed, straw and topdressing runs $0.40–$0.80 per sq ft all-in in most markets; sod installed runs $1.50–$3.00. I'll use $0.60 for seed.

Only the machine-dependent tasks are counted: cutting, dressing the bottom to fall, backfilling, handling spoil, and restoration. Layout, fabric, stone, pipe and the outfall are the same work regardless, so leaving them out doesn't change the comparison.

Two things fall out of that.

First, the mini excavator is slower in the cut but cheaper in labour than you'd guess, because backfill and spoil handling with a bucket are quick. It loses on total cost, and it loses on restoration, not on speed.

Second, restoration is doing most of the damage. Swap seed for sod at $2.00 per sq ft and the numbers move hard: the walk-behind restoration goes from $270 to $900 and the mini goes from $720 to $2,400. Total spread widens from about $670 to about $1,100 on one 150-foot run.

On a job bidding somewhere in the $6,000–$9,000 range, that spread is roughly 8 to 15 percent of contract. That's a decision worth making on purpose.

Change one variable and the answer flips

The table above says walk-behind, and for that exact job it's right. It is also the most fragile answer of the three, because the walk-behind is the machine most affected by everything the yard hasn't told you yet.

Heavy wet clay. Drop the walk-behind from 70 to 35 ft/hr and the cut goes from 2.15 hr to 4.30 hr. That's 2.15 extra hours at $120 = $258, taking it to roughly $1,416. The mini barely moves — clay digs fine with a bucket, it just sticks. The gap closes from $670 to about $415.

Depth to 36 inches. Volume goes up 50 percent for every machine, but only the walk-behind is near the end of its boom. A machine rated 36 inches in a catalogue often makes 30 in real soil once the chain is loaded. Verify the boom on the actual unit before you commit a depth in writing.

Corners. Each direction change on a trencher means backing out, repositioning, and hand-squaring the intersection. Budget 20 to 30 minutes each. Two 90-degree turns is an hour of crew time, roughly $120, and the mini does them for free.

A pit anywhere in the run. A catch basin sump, a dry well, a pump chamber, a headwall footing — a trencher digs none of them. A 3 × 3 × 4 ft pit is 1.33 bank yards, and by shovel that's three to five crew-hours with two people. One pit erases the walk-behind's entire advantage on this job.

Roots. One 3-inch root stops a chain and costs 10 to 20 minutes of saw work with the machine idle. Four roots and you've lost the morning.

What no machine digs

Three things stay hand work no matter what's parked in the driveway, and all three belong in the estimate as named lines rather than as surprises.

Utility crossings. Call your one-call service and get the locates before anything with a chain touches dirt. Most states require you to hand-expose within a tolerance zone either side of the mark — commonly 18 to 24 inches, but the number and the rule are state-specific, so read yours rather than trusting a number from a blog. Budget 30 to 60 minutes per crossing and carry a shovel and a probe, not a bucket.

Roots. Anything over about 2 inches near a mature tree gets cut clean with a saw, not torn with a machine. Torn roots are how you end up replacing a customer's maple two summers later.

Rock. If you hit ledge or cobble mid-run, that's a scope change, priced and signed before the crew restarts — not something you absorb to look tough. A mini with a hammer turns some rock into a solvable problem, but the hammer is another rental line and another day.

Then there's the damage the customer actually notices. Lay plywood or mats on the entry route, tarp the spoil instead of piling it on grass, keep to one entry path, and sweep the walk before you leave. On a wet-ground job those four habits do more for your reviews than the machine choice does.

A decision rule you can run from the driveway

Work down this list on the site visit and stop at the first line that matches.

  1. Trench deeper than 36 inches anywhere in the run — mini excavator.
  2. Any pit: catch basin sump, dry well, pump chamber, structure footing — mini excavator, or price the hand dig honestly at three to five crew-hours per pit.
  3. More than two direction changes, or a curved run following a swale — mini excavator.
  4. Known roots over 2 inches, cobble, or fill with debris in it — mini excavator plus hand tools. A chain is the wrong tool and you'll pay for it twice.
  5. Narrowest access point under 36 inches and the fence can't come out — walk-behind, or a 1-ton class mini measured retracted.
  6. Straight run over about 250 feet in diggable soil with wide access — ride-on trencher. Its speed advantage only pays back the rental and delivery over distance.
  7. Everything else — straight, under 36 inches, diggable soil, tight-ish gate — walk-behind, because restoration is the biggest variable line and the narrow trench wins it.

One override sits on top of all seven. If the lawn is the thing the customer is proud of, weight the restoration number heavier than the rental number, every time. A $300 rental saving that costs you 750 extra square feet of sod is not a saving.

Find out whether you guessed right

Every figure above is a starting point, including the ones I derived. Your soil, your crew, your rental yard and your restoration sub will all move them.

The only way to replace them with real numbers is to write down four things on your next three digs: machine and rental cost, elapsed hours on the trench, spoil yards actually moved, and square feet of turf you put back. FAQ

Can a walk-behind trencher dig 24 inches deep?

Most rental walk-behinds are rated somewhere between 18 and 36 inches depending on boom length, so 24 inches is well within range for the mid and larger units. The catch is that a catalogue depth assumes soil that cuts cleanly. In heavy clay or compacted fill the practical depth is often a few inches less than the rating, so check the boom on the actual unit you're reserving rather than the model line.

What size mini excavator fits through a 36-inch gate?

A 1 to 1.5 ton machine with retractable tracks typically measures 28 to 39 inches retracted, so the smaller end of that class will fit a true 36-inch opening. Check the blade width as well as the track width, because on some machines the blade is the wider dimension. Also measure the tightest point past the gate, which is usually the AC pad or the gas meter, not the gate itself.

How many feet per hour will a trencher dig in clay?

In workable soil a walk-behind will usually run 60 to 120 feet per hour at drainage depths, and a compact ride-on 150 to 300. In heavy wet clay expect that to drop by roughly half — 25 to 50 for a walk-behind, 80 to 150 for a ride-on. A mini excavator is the least affected by clay, holding somewhere around 30 to 50 feet per hour either way, which is why the machines converge on bad soil.

Is it cheaper to rent a mini excavator or a trencher for a French drain?

On a short, straight, shallow run in diggable soil, a walk-behind trencher is usually cheaper overall — not because the rental is less, but because the narrow trench cuts the restoration bill roughly in half. On deeper runs, runs with corners, or any job with a basin or dry well pit, the mini excavator wins. Run the numbers with your own rental rate, loaded crew cost and restoration price per square foot rather than trusting a rule of thumb.

Do I ever need both a trencher and a mini excavator on the same job?

Yes, and it's more common than contractors expect. A long straight main run with a dry well at the end is the classic case: trench the run in an hour, then have the mini dig the pit, set the chamber and backfill. Two rentals and two deliveries only make sense if the run is long enough that the trencher saves more hours than the second delivery costs, so price it both ways before you book.

The short version

Trench width drives restoration, and restoration is usually the biggest variable cost in the machine decision. On a 150-foot run at 24 inches in diggable soil, a 6-inch walk-behind beat a 12-inch mini excavator by around $670 in a worked example, almost entirely on turf replaced. Flip to the mini the moment you're deeper than 36 inches, turning more than two corners, digging any pit, or hitting roots and rock — and budget hand work for every utility crossing regardless of what you rent.
