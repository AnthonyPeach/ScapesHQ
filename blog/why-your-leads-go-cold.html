Why Your Leads Go Cold After the First Call

Forty leads a month, called back in under two minutes, and more than half of them still never hear from you a second time.

September 13, 2026 · 7 min read

Forty leads came in last month. You called thirty-eight of them back inside five minutes, because you already fixed that part. Eighteen answered. The other twenty-two got one voicemail and then nothing, and by Friday they were off the whiteboard.

Those twenty-two didn't go cold. They went unattended. There's a difference, and the difference is worth real money.

This picks up where speed to lead leaves off. Getting to the phone in two minutes is the single highest-leverage thing you can fix — and it only decides what happens on the first attempt. Everything after that is cadence.

The gap isn't speed anymore, it's the second touch

Most contractors have one follow-up habit: call back fast, leave a voicemail, and if the homeowner calls back, great. If not, the lead sits in the inbox until it gets scrolled past.

That's not laziness. It's that the second touch has no trigger. The first one does — your phone buzzes, you call. Nothing buzzes on day four.

Homeowners who fill out a drainage form are rarely ignoring you on purpose. They filled out three forms on a Sunday night after the yard held water, then Monday happened. The one who calls them Thursday with something specific to say is usually the only one who called Thursday at all.

Price the follow-up before you decide it's not worth it

Run your own version of this. Here's the shape of it with the numbers from the top of the page.

Forty leads a month. Eighteen reached on the first attempt. Twenty-two that got a voicemail and nothing else.

Reach rates on a worked cadence vary widely by market, season and lead source — a paid Meta lead after a week of rain behaves nothing like the same form fill in a dry September. So take a conservative number and let the reader be you: assume a real cadence turns one in four of those unreached leads into an actual conversation.

22 × 0.25 = 5 or 6 conversations you weren't having.

Some of those are tire-kickers, out of area, or already hired someone. Say half survive to a booked site visit: 3 visits. Close two of three, which is normal for drainage work where you've done a real diagnostic: 2 jobs. At a $5,500 average ticket, that's $11,000 a month.

Now the cost side. Six touches per lead, roughly two minutes each once you're not writing from scratch: 22 × 5 remaining touches × 2 minutes = 220 minutes, about 3.7 hours a month.

Halve every assumption you don't like. One job instead of two, $4,000 ticket instead of $5,500. You're still trading under four hours of phone and typing for four thousand dollars. And you already paid for those leads — at $1,400 of ad spend across 40 leads, the twenty-two you dropped cost you $770 to generate.

Substitute your own lead count, your own reach rate and your own ticket. The arithmetic almost never comes out against following up.

Six touches over fourteen days, then the file closes

Persistence stops working when it stops being about the homeowner's yard and starts being about your pipeline. The line is roughly this: every touch should give them a reason to reply that isn't "checking in."

Six touches over two weeks does that. More than that inside two weeks reads as pressure. Fewer than that and you're beaten by anyone who bothered.

Then stop. One seasonal re-open later — before spring thaw in cold markets, before the wet season in warm ones — is fair. A monthly "just following up" forever is not, and it trains people to ignore your name.

Vary the times of day. If touches 1, 3 and 5 all land at 8:15 a.m., you've really only tested one time slot three times.

Every touch has to carry something the last one didn't

The reason follow-up feels like nagging is that most of it is content-free. "Just checking in on that estimate" adds nothing, so it earns nothing. Here's what each touch can actually carry.

  • Touch 2 — two real windows. "I'm in your area Thursday morning and Friday after 2. Either of those work to come look at the low spot by the patio?" Two options is a decision. "Let me know when works" is homework.
  • Touch 3 — one question. "Quick question before I come out — does the water sit after every rain, or only the heavy ones?" A question with a one-sentence answer gets replies that an offer doesn't. It also tells you something you need.
  • Touch 4 — proof, with a number. Two photos from a job with the same symptom, one line on what it involved, one line on what it cost. Homeowners are usually silent because they have no idea if this is a $900 problem or a $9,000 problem. Answer that and they'll tell you where they stand.
  • Touch 5 — honest scheduling. "We're booking about three weeks out now, and I've got a crew on your street the week of the 20th." Only say it if it's true. Fake urgency in drainage work gets caught, because the neighbor talks.
  • Touch 6 — the close-out. "I'm going to stop chasing you — I don't want to be the guy who won't quit. If the yard's still holding water after the next storm, call me and I'll come look." This one gets more replies than touches 3, 4 and 5 combined in most contractors' experience. People respond to being let go.

On voicemail, short beats polished:

"Dave with Watershed, calling about the standing water behind your garage. I've got Thursday morning open. Call or text this number back, it's my cell."

Fourteen seconds. Names the problem, gives a slot, tells them how to reach you. If your voicemail runs forty seconds, most people hang up before the part that matters.

One more thing about touch 2: if you charge for diagnostics, say so here, not on day nine. Burying the fee until they're invested feels like a bait and switch. There's a script for saying it cleanly in what to charge for a drainage site visit.

A quiet lead and a quiet proposal are different problems

Everything above is for a lead you haven't met. Once you've walked the yard and sent a number, the silence means something else, and the cadence changes.

A pre-visit lead may not remember you exist. A post-proposal homeowner remembers you fine. They're comparing bids, waiting on a spouse, or stuck on a line item they don't understand and don't want to look dumb asking about.

So the post-proposal cadence is shorter and more specific. Day 2: "Any questions on the restoration line?" Day 5: a call. Day 10: "Are we still in it, or did you go another direction? Either answer is fine — I just want to know whether to hold the slot." Then close the file.

The thing that kills post-proposal follow-up is not knowing if they opened it. When you send a PDF attachment you're following up blind, which is why online proposals close faster — a proposal you can see was viewed twice on Sunday night gets a completely different call than one that was never opened.

The cadence lives or dies on where you write it down

None of this survives a busy week in your head. Touch 4 on day 4 requires something on day 4 telling you it's touch 4.

Whatever you use, it needs three things: the next action, the date it's due, and what the last touch actually said so you don't repeat yourself. A paper day planner does this. A calendar reminder with the lead's name in it does this. A shared sheet does this until two people are editing it.

Be clear-eyed about what your tools do. ScapesHQ keeps a permanent activity timeline and send history on every client record, so you can see what went out and when before you dial. It does not have automated follow-up sequences, SMS to customers, or reminder playbooks — those aren't built, and if you need the system to nudge you on day 9, that nudge has to come from your calendar or your phone for now.

That's not a small gap, and pretending otherwise would waste your time. What a record does give you is the thing most follow-up gets wrong: walking into touch 5 knowing exactly what you said in touches 1 through 4.

FAQ

How many times should I follow up with a lead before giving up?

Six touches over about fourteen days, then stop and close the file. A single seasonal re-open months later is fair — before spring thaw in cold markets, before the wet season in warm ones. Anything beyond that starts training the homeowner to ignore your name.

Is texting a homeowner who filled out a form okay?

Generally yes if they gave you the number on a form asking about their yard, since that's an inquiry you're responding to. Rules vary by state and country, and consent language on your lead form matters, so check what applies in your market. Keep it identifiable: your name, your company, and the specific problem they described.

What should I say in a follow-up voicemail?

Under fifteen seconds. Your name and company, the specific problem from their form, one concrete time you could come out, and how to reach you. Long voicemails get deleted before the part that matters, and generic ones get deleted immediately.

How is post-proposal follow-up different from following up on a new lead?

A new lead may not remember filling out your form. A homeowner sitting on your proposal remembers you fine and is comparing bids, waiting on a spouse, or stuck on a line item. So the cadence is shorter — day 2, day 5, day 10 — and each touch should ask about something specific in the document rather than checking in.

Does ScapesHQ send follow-up reminders or automated sequences?

No. Automated playbooks, reminder sequences and SMS to customers are not built. What it does keep is a permanent activity timeline and send history on each record, so you can see exactly what was sent and when before your next call — but the reminder itself has to come from your calendar or phone.

The short version

Speed to lead only decides the first attempt; most lost work dies between touch one and touch two. Run six touches over fourteen days — call, same-day text with two windows, next-morning question, day-4 proof with a price, day-9 scheduling call, day-14 close-out — and make sure each one carries something the last didn't. Do the arithmetic on your own unreached leads before deciding it's not worth the hours: at 22 dropped leads a month and a $5,500 average ticket, one recovered job pays for the whole habit many times over.
