Corrugated vs PVC Drain Pipe: Settling It With Numbers

A four-year-old single-wall run under a widened driveway, flattened to an oval and impossible to snake, and the arithmetic that would have prevented it.

September 23, 2026 · 11 min read

A contractor I trade notes with pulled a 40-foot length of 4-inch single-wall out of a front yard last spring. It had been in the ground four years, about 14 inches of cover, carrying roof water out to a daylight in the ditch line. Eighteen months after it went in, the homeowner had the concrete driveway widened over part of the run.

Where the delivery trucks crossed, the pipe was an oval maybe half its original height, silted to the crown, and there was no getting a snake past the pinch. The fix was a new run and a section of saw-cut concrete nobody had budgeted for.

Nothing stupid happened here. The pipe was adequate for what was on top of it the day it was installed, and then what was on top of it changed. That's the real pipe argument — not corrugated versus PVC as a matter of loyalty, but whether the pipe matches the load, the fall and the maintenance you can reasonably expect over the next fifteen years.

A 100-foot coil of 4-inch single-wall costs roughly what two 10-foot sticks of Schedule 40 cost in most markets. That ratio is why the conversation never ends, and it's also why the conversation is usually had wrong — on price per foot instead of price per bid.

The four pipes actually on the shelf, and the one that gets confused for another

There are four common choices plus one impostor, and the differences that matter are interior wall, joint type and whether the wall carries load on its own or leans on the soil around it.

Those per-foot figures are ranges, not prices. Resin moves, freight moves, and a yard in Dallas and a yard in Spokane will not quote you the same number this week. Call your supplier and write their numbers in the margin before you use any of the arithmetic further down.

The impostor is the thin-wall belled PVC sold in 10-foot sticks at most big-box stores. It is smooth inside, which is its one real advantage, but the wall is a fraction of SDR-35 and the joints are not gasketed. Plenty of contractors buy it thinking they bought sewer pipe. If the label says D2729, you bought drain pipe, not sewer pipe.

Crush rating is a cover-depth question, not a pipe question

All of these are flexible pipe to one degree or another. They don't resist load the way cast iron does; they deflect, and the soil beside them pushes back. That means the number you care about isn't a single crush figure printed on the pipe. It's the combination of wall stiffness, how well you bedded and compacted the sides, and how much cover you have.

Single-wall gets its strength almost entirely from the envelope around it. Bedded in washed stone, wrapped in fabric, three feet down under a lawn, it holds shape fine — agricultural drainage has run on it for decades under tractors, at depth. Put it 12 inches under a lawn a pickup will occasionally cross and it ovals. Manufacturers do not publish a highway-load rating for it, and that absence is the answer.

Dual-wall HDPE and SDR-35 both have real published pipe stiffness and both are rated for vehicle loading — but only at or above a stated minimum cover, commonly around a foot for small diameters. Below that minimum the rating doesn't apply. Pull the cover table for the brand you actually buy; they differ, and they differ by diameter.

Schedule 40 is the one that behaves closest to a rigid pipe. That's why it's what goes under slabs and in shallow-cover situations where you can't get a foot of dirt over the crown. It is also the most brittle of the four in cold weather and the least forgiving of a rock left in the bedding.

A corrugated interior costs you half your capacity at the same slope

This is the part that gets skipped, and it's the part with clean arithmetic behind it.

Gravity flow in a pipe runs on Manning's equation, and for a given diameter and slope the flow you get is inversely proportional to the roughness coefficient n. Smooth-wall PVC and the smooth interior of dual-wall run about n = 0.010 to 0.012. Single-wall corrugated is published around n = 0.020 to 0.024.

Double the roughness, halve the flow. Same 4-inch pipe, same 1 percent fall, and the corrugated run carries roughly half what the smooth run carries.

Run it the other way and it gets starker. Slope enters the equation as a square root, so flow goes with the square root of fall. To make up for doubling n, you'd need four times the slope. On a 150-foot run, 1 percent is 1.5 feet of fall. Four times that is 6 feet. On most residential lots you don't have 6 feet to give.

You can buy the capacity back with diameter instead. Going from 4-inch to 6-inch multiplies cross-sectional area by 2.25 and improves the hydraulic radius on top of that, which lands you near three times the flow at the same roughness. So 6-inch single-wall genuinely does move more water than 4-inch SDR-35.

Price 6-inch single-wall, though, and the savings mostly evaporate. You also just widened your trench and doubled your stone. If you're sizing pipe off roof area for downspout work, the numbers in sizing pipe from roof area assume you know which roughness you're working with — plug in corrugated where you assumed smooth and the run is undersized on paper before you dig.

Whether you can jet it decides what the pipe is worth in year eight

Every drain line silts eventually. The question is whether year eight is a service call you can bill or an excavation you have to apologise for.

A smooth-wall line with a cleanout at the head and a known outfall is a 90-minute job with a jetter. A corrugated line is a different animal. The nozzle catches on every rib, the cable on a snake does the same, and root cutters shred the thin wall. High-pressure water will also find its way out of snap couplers, which were never watertight to begin with. Some contractors jet single-wall successfully at low pressure with the right nozzle. Nobody does it twice on the same line and feels good about it.

Corrugations also hold silt and standing water between ribs by design. Sediment that would flush through a smooth pipe stays put. That's one of the mechanisms behind the five ways French drains fail — the fabric silts, the flow slows, and the pipe that can't be cleaned becomes the pipe that gets replaced.

There's a fitting problem too. Single-wall has no proper cleanout. You can adapt to a PVC tee and riser, and that adapter is usually the weakest joint on the whole run. If you're installing a system you intend to service, the service access wants to be PVC regardless of what the rest of the run is.

What the upgrade actually adds to a 150-foot bid

Here's the arithmetic on a 150-foot solid 4-inch run under a lawn: two 45s, one wye, one basin adapter, one cleanout tee. Substitute your own supplier prices and your own crew cost.

I'll use the middle of the ranges in the table: $0.80 a foot single-wall, $2.30 dual-wall, $2.60 SDR-35, $4.50 Schedule 40. Fittings at about $6 each for corrugated snap couplers, $15 for gasketed or DWV PVC, $20 for the Schedule 40 set.

  • Single-wall: 150 × $0.80 = $120, plus 5 fittings × $6 = $30. $150
  • Dual-wall N-12: 150 × $2.30 = $345, plus 5 × $15 = $75. $420
  • SDR-35: 150 × $2.60 = $390, plus 5 × $15 = $75. $465
  • Schedule 40: 150 × $4.50 = $675, plus 5 × $20 = $100. $775

Then labor. A coil walks out in one piece. A hundred and fifty feet of 10-foot sticks is fifteen joints to cut, clean, lubricate or glue, plus more care in the bedding because a rigid joint over a void is where a pipe cracks. Call it two extra crew-hours for a two-person crew. Use your own loaded hourly crew cost — the real one, wages plus burden plus equipment, not the wage on the check.

At $110 an hour for a two-person crew, that's $220. So the single-wall-to-SDR-35 upgrade costs you $315 in material and $220 in labor. $535 at cost. Marked up the way you normally mark up material and billed the way you normally bill labor, it shows up on the customer's number somewhere under $700.

Set that against the 148-foot job in the French drain breakdown, which came out at $13,130. Seven hundred dollars is about five percent of that bid. The Schedule 40 version is roughly $850 at cost, call it $1,050 billed — about eight percent.

Five percent. That's the entire financial content of the pipe argument on a job that size. It is smaller than the rock allowance, smaller than the restoration line, and smaller than the gap between you and the cheap bid down the street. Once you've seen that number you stop treating pipe as the place to save money.

A decision rule based on what's on top of the pipe

Ask what will be over this pipe in fifteen years, not what's over it today. Then work down the list.

  1. Under lawn, foot traffic and a mower, 18 inches or more of cover, perforated collector in a stone envelope. Single-wall is defensible. The stone carries the load and the pipe is a collector, not a conveyor.
  2. Under lawn, but trucks, trailers or a skid steer cross it, or cover is under 18 inches. Dual-wall minimum. Confirm the manufacturer's cover requirement for the diameter you're running.
  3. Under a driveway, apron or parking area. Dual-wall or SDR-35, at or above the published minimum cover. If you can't get that cover, sleeve the run in a larger Schedule 40 or steel casing under the pavement.
  4. Under a patio, walk or anything hardscaped, now or planned. Smooth wall, no exceptions, with a cleanout at each end. You will never dig this out without demolishing something, so it has to be serviceable from the surface.
  5. Under a slab, inside the building footprint, or tied into a sanitary or storm connection. Schedule 40 or whatever the code official says. This isn't a preference question, and the inspector settles it.
  6. Any run with less than 12 inches of cover, anywhere. Schedule 40, or re-route so you get cover.

The fourth one is where the money is, and it's a question you ask on the site visit, not in the truck afterward. "Any plans for a patio or a wider drive back here?" takes four seconds. Half the time the answer is yes, and now your pipe spec has a reason the homeowner understands rather than a reason they have to take on faith.

Write the spec on the proposal by name — pipe type, diameter, cleanout locations. Not "drain pipe." If you keep a template line for it, the decision gets made once and stops getting remade in the parking lot of the supply house. ScapesHQ estimates and proposals carry template line items for exactly that reason. It doesn't do job costing, so whether the upgrade actually held your margin is still something you check by hand on the next three jobs.

Where single-wall is genuinely the right call

Plenty of people talk about single-wall like it's a tell that a contractor doesn't know better. That's lazy. It has real applications and pretending otherwise makes you sound like you're upselling.

It's the right pipe for a perforated curtain drain or interceptor under a lawn, bedded in washed stone with adequate cover, where the job is collecting groundwater across a long run rather than conveying a concentrated flow. Flow per foot is low, the envelope carries the load, and you're not going to jet a collector anyway — you'd flush the stone.

It's the right pipe when the route is tight and irregular. A coil bends around a mature tree's root plate in one continuous piece, where PVC would need six fittings and six potential leak points, and each of those fittings is a place for roots to find a seam.

It's the right pipe for temporary work. Diverting construction runoff for a season, dewatering a dig, a stopgap on a rental property where the owner has told you plainly what the budget is. Say out loud that it's the economy option, put the life expectancy in writing, and you've sold honestly.

And it's the right pipe at depth, well away from vehicle loads, on a long run to daylight where you have plenty of fall to spend and can size up a diameter to cover the friction penalty.

What single-wall isn't is the default. The default should be smooth-wall, because five percent of the bid buys you a line you can service in year eight, and because the driveway the homeowner hasn't mentioned yet is the single most common reason a good drainage install turns into a callback you eat.

FAQ

Can I run single-wall corrugated under a driveway if I bury it deep enough?

Manufacturers don't publish a vehicle-load rating for single-wall, so there's no depth at which you can point to a spec and say it's covered. Depth and good side compaction genuinely help, because flexible pipe leans on the soil around it, but you're working without a number. Use dual-wall or SDR-35 at the published minimum cover, or sleeve the crossing in larger Schedule 40.

Is SDR-35 or Schedule 40 better for a French drain?

For a buried gravity line with a foot or more of cover, SDR-35 is plenty strong and costs less per foot, and the gasketed bell means no glue. Schedule 40 earns its extra cost where cover is shallow, where the run is under a slab, or where code requires it. On a typical lawn or driveway French drain, SDR-35 is the better value.

Does a jetter damage corrugated drain pipe?

It can. The nozzle catches on the ribs, high pressure finds its way out of snap couplers that were never watertight, and root cutters tear the thin wall of single-wall. Some contractors jet it successfully at low pressure with a skid nozzle, but it isn't a line you can plan on servicing that way. If the run needs to be jettable in ten years, put in smooth wall and a cleanout.

How much more does smooth-wall pipe add to a drainage bid?

Work it yourself: length times the per-foot delta at your supplier, plus fittings, plus a couple of crew-hours for the extra joints and bedding care. On a 150-foot 4-inch run with mid-range pricing, single-wall to SDR-35 came out around $535 at cost in the worked example above, roughly five percent of a $13,000 job once marked up. Your supplier prices and crew rate will shift that, so run the arithmetic with your own numbers.

What's the difference between SDR-35 and the belled PVC drain pipe at the big-box store?

The store pipe is usually ASTM D2729 thin-wall sewer and drain, with a much thinner wall than SDR-35 and friction-fit rather than gasketed bells. It's smooth inside, which helps flow, but it isn't a sewer-rated pipe and it won't take the loads SDR-35 will. Check the stamp on the pipe rather than the aisle it came from.

The short version

Pipe choice comes down to what will be on top of the run in fifteen years, not what's on top of it today. Single-wall is defensible for perforated collectors under a lawn with real cover and a stone envelope; anything under a driveway, a patio, a slab, or less than a foot of cover wants smooth wall with cleanouts. On a 150-foot 4-inch run, upgrading from single-wall to SDR-35 penciled out around $535 at cost — about five percent of a $13,000 job — so price both and put the spec on the proposal by name.
