"The Other Guy Is $3,000 Cheaper": Holding Your Price

A $3,030 gap between two drainage bids is almost never a $3,030 gap in profit — it's usually a gap in pipe, stone, depth or restoration that nobody wrote down.

September 13, 2026 · 9 min read

You sent a $13,130 proposal for a 148-foot French drain on Tuesday. Friday afternoon the homeowner calls and says the other guy quoted $10,100.

Three thousand and thirty dollars. Said out loud like that, it sounds like you're the expensive one.

You're probably not. You're almost certainly looking at two different jobs with the same words on the cover page. The work in the next ten minutes isn't defending your number — it's finding out what the other number buys.

The two bids are rarely the same job, and the homeowner can't tell

A homeowner comparing drainage bids is comparing two pieces of paper that both say "French drain." They have no way to know that one of them means 12 inches deep with corrugated flex pipe and native backfill, and the other means 24 inches to positive fall, rigid perforated pipe, washed stone by the ton, and sod over the trench.

Those are different jobs. One of them fails in three springs. The homeowner has no vocabulary for that difference until you give them one.

So the first move is not "here's why I'm worth more." The first move is to find out what you're actually being compared to. If you skip this, every sentence after it is a guess.

Three questions that surface the difference in ninety seconds

Ask these on the phone, in this order, in a curious tone rather than a defensive one. You're not cross-examining the homeowner. You're helping them read a document they can't read yet.

  1. "How deep are they digging, and where does the water come out?" Depth and outfall are the two things that decide whether the system works. If the answer is "it doesn't say" or "he didn't mention," you've learned most of what you need.
  2. "What pipe and what stone — does it list a type and a quantity?" A bid that says "pipe and gravel as needed" is not a bid. A bid that says "4-inch rigid perforated, sock, 20 tons of washed 3/4-inch" is a real one and deserves respect.
  3. "Does their price include putting the yard back?" Restoration is the single most common line missing from a cheap bid. Spoil haul-off is second. Together they can easily account for a four-figure gap on a job this size, and the homeowner will pay for both eventually either way.

You are allowed to ask to see the other quote. Plenty of homeowners will read it to you or send a photo. Say it plainly: "If you can send me their scope, I'll tell you honestly whether it's the same job. If it is, take it — that's a good price." That sentence costs you nothing and buys you enormous credibility.

Where the three thousand usually hides

On a mid-size drainage job, a gap of 20-30% between two honest contractors is unusual. A gap that size almost always traces to specific lines. Here's where to look first — the dollar figures move a lot by market, so put your own in.

If you built your number properly — every bucket accounted for, the way we walked through in pricing a 148-foot French drain line by line — you can say exactly what each of your lines costs, which is something the other contractor generally can't do.

What matching that price actually costs you

Run this before you decide anything. Use your own numbers; I'll use the $13,130 job to show the shape.

Say your direct costs on that job — crew hours, machine, pipe, stone, fabric, haul-off, restoration materials, dump fees — come to $8,900. Gross profit is $13,130 − $8,900 = $4,230, or about 32% of the price.

Now match at $10,100. Your costs don't move. Gross profit becomes $10,100 − $8,900 = $1,200, about 12%.

That's the part homeowners never see: a 23% price cut took 72% of your profit. Every dollar off the price comes off profit, not off cost.

Then the real number. To produce the same $4,230 at the discounted price, you'd need $4,230 ÷ $1,200 = 3.5 of those jobs. Three and a half times the crew days, the fuel, the wear, the risk of hitting rock, the callbacks — for the same money you'd have made on one.

Do this arithmetic once with your own cost number and the objection stops feeling like a negotiation. It's just a question of whether you want to work three and a half times as hard.

One caveat worth saying out loud: this only works if you know your real direct cost, and most contractors are working off an estimate, not actuals. ScapesHQ doesn't track job actuals or cost jobs against budget — that isn't built — so the honest source for that $8,900 is your own invoices, tickets and payroll from the last three jobs like it.

Scripts for holding price without arguing

The tone that works is calm and slightly on the homeowner's side. You are not defending yourself. You are helping them avoid a bad purchase, and if they buy it anyway, that's allowed.

When they tell you the number:

"That's a real price for a real scope — I'm not going to tell you he's wrong. What I'd want to know if it were my house is how deep he's going and where the water ends up. Does his quote say?"

When the other bid turns out to be thinner:

"Here's the difference, as best I can tell. My price includes hauling the spoil off and sodding the trench, and his doesn't mention either. That's not him being cheap — it's a different job. If you want, I'll price mine without restoration so you're comparing like for like, and you can handle the lawn yourself."

That last sentence is the one to practice. You're not discounting. You're removing scope. A lower price with less work in it protects your margin and teaches the homeowner what things cost.

When they ask you flat out to match:

"I can't get to that number and do it the way I've described. What I can do is show you where every dollar goes, and if you still want the other bid, take it — no hard feelings. If it doesn't hold up in a year, call me and we'll talk about what's left to fix."

When they go quiet after that, don't chase with a discount. Chase with information, on the schedule laid out in the day 1, 3 and 7 follow-up sequence. A price cut offered unprompted on day 3 tells them your first number was padded.

Three cases where walking away is the correct answer

Not every objection is a closing problem. Some of these jobs should go to the other guy.

  • Matching would mean building something you don't believe in. If the only way to reach $10,100 is to dig shallower than the fall requires or skip the outfall work, you're putting your name on a system that fails. The callback, the argument and the review all land on you, not on the price.
  • The discount drops you below your walk-away number. Set that number before the call, not during it. A simple version: the price below which the job returns less gross profit per crew day than your average. If matching puts you under it, the answer is no, and it's no regardless of how slow next week looks.
  • The buyer is shopping on price and only price. You can spot this: no questions about method, no interest in the site visit findings, a fourth and fifth bid in progress, and pressure to "just give me your best number." That client will also fight you about the change order when you hit rock at 18 inches. Let them go early, while it's free.

Walking away should be said out loud and kindly. "I don't think I'm your guy on this one, and I mean that in a friendly way." A surprising number of those people call back in two weeks.

Most price objections are proposal problems that showed up late

If you get this objection on more than a third of your bids, the fix isn't better scripting. It's that your proposal let the homeowner compare on price because it gave them nothing else to compare on.

Three things move that number. First, write depth, pipe type, stone quantity, outfall and restoration as separate, plain-language lines — so a thin competing bid looks thin next to yours instead of looking cheap. Second, give two or three options at different scopes rather than one take-it-or-leave-it price, so the conversation becomes "which one" instead of "how much less." Third, make signing take seconds, because a proposal that sits for a week collects other bids. The structure for all three is in the drainage proposal that gets signed the same week.

FAQ

Should I ask to see the other contractor's quote?

Yes, and ask for it openly. Frame it as help rather than suspicion: tell the homeowner that if the scopes match, they should take the lower price. Most people will read it to you or send a photo, and the missing lines usually show up in under a minute.

What do I say when a customer asks me to match a lower bid?

Don't cut the price — cut the scope. Offer to reprice without the lines the other bid is missing, like restoration or spoil haul-off, so the two numbers describe the same work. That protects your margin and teaches the homeowner what each piece actually costs.

How much can I discount before the job isn't worth doing?

Work it out before the call. Subtract your direct costs from the price to get gross profit, then divide that by the crew days the job takes. Your walk-away price is the one where profit per crew day drops below your average — below that line you're funding the customer's project with your own time.

Is it worth losing money on a first job to win a new customer?

Rarely in drainage. Repeat work on a drainage fix is thin by nature, and a customer acquired on price tends to shop on price again next time. If you want to invest in a relationship, do it with free diagnostics or faster response, not with a discounted install.

What if the cheaper bid is from a bigger, established company?

Ask the same three questions — depth, pipe and stone, restoration. Size doesn't change physics, and larger outfits sometimes price thin scopes intentionally and make it back on change orders. If their scope genuinely matches yours at a lower price, that's a real signal about your own cost structure, and it's worth checking your rates rather than arguing.

The short version

When a homeowner says the other guy is $3,000 cheaper, find out what the other bid includes before you say anything about your own price — depth, pipe and stone, outfall, spoil and restoration are where the gap almost always hides. If you match, you're giving up profit dollar for dollar: on a $13,130 job with $8,900 in direct costs, dropping to $10,100 takes 72% of your gross profit and means running three and a half jobs to earn what one used to. Remove scope instead of cutting price, and walk away when matching would mean building something you don't believe in.
