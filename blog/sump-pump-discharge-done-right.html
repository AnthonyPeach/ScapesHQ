Sump Pump Discharge Done Right: Where the Water Goes

A sump pump that moves 30 gallons a minute into a frozen pipe twelve feet from the house is a call-back with a date on it.

September 13, 2026 · 10 min read

A homeowner calls in early February. Basement's wet again, pump's running, they can hear it. You drive out, pull the cover, and the pump is cycling every ninety seconds against a solid plug of ice about eight feet down the discharge line.

Nothing is broken. The pump works. The pit works. The check valve works. The only thing wrong is where the water was asked to go, and that decision was made the day the line was buried.

Discharge is the cheapest part of a sump job to install and the most expensive part to get wrong. The pump is a known quantity with a nameplate on it. The pit is a hole. The discharge is the part where judgment shows up, and it's the part that brings you back in the cold.

The discharge line has one job: put water somewhere it can't come back

Everything else follows from that. Not "away from the house" — water that goes twelve feet and then sits in a low spot is water that's coming back through the same footing drain it just left. You're paying to run the pump in a circle.

So before you pick a pipe size or a burial depth, walk the discharge route with a level and answer four things:

  • Where's the lowest point on the route? If it's between the house and the termination, you have a belly, and a belly holds water that freezes.
  • How much fall do you have from the house wall to the intended outlet? Measure it, don't eyeball it. On a flat lot you may have four inches over sixty feet.
  • What does the soil at the outlet actually do with water? A dry well at the end of a sump line is a different calculation from a dry well handling roof water, because the sump runs when the ground is already saturated.
  • Where does the water go if the outlet is blocked, frozen or submerged? There's always an answer. Usually it's "back toward the house." Your job is to make it something else.

That last one is the whole post in one line. Design the discharge for the day it doesn't work, not the day it does.

Distance from the foundation is a soil question, not a number you memorize

You'll see ten feet quoted constantly. Twenty feet also gets quoted. Both are shorthand, and both are wrong often enough to hurt you.

The real question is how far the water has to travel before the soil under it stops being part of the disturbed backfill around your foundation. Backfill is looser than undisturbed soil. It drinks. Discharging onto backfill is discharging into the footing drain with extra steps.

Excavation around a full basement typically extends two to four feet beyond the wall, sometimes more where they needed room to work. Add the slope you need to carry water away from that zone, and the practical minimum lands in the ten-to-fifteen foot range for most residential lots — further on tight clay where nothing infiltrates, potentially less where you're terminating into a proper daylight outfall on a slope.

Here's the arithmetic that matters more than the distance. Take a 1/3 HP pump moving roughly 35 gallons per minute at 10 feet of head. A pit that cycles four times an hour for thirty seconds a cycle is:

35 gpm × 0.5 min × 4 cycles = 70 gallons per hour

Over a 24-hour rain event that's 1,680 gallons. Call it 225 cubic feet of water. If you're dumping that within the backfill zone on clay soil, you haven't drained the basement — you've built a recirculating loop and charged the homeowner for the privilege.

Run your own version of that. Time one cycle with a stopwatch on the site visit, count the cycles in ten minutes during a wet week, and you have the homeowner's actual number instead of a pump nameplate.

Three termination options, and what each one actually costs you later

Most sump discharge ends up in one of three places. They're not interchangeable, and the cheapest install is rarely the cheapest over five years.

TerminationWorks whenFails whenCall-back risk
Daylight outfall on a slopeYou have real fall — 2% or better to a spot well below the pit and off the backfillOutlet buried by mulch, mowed over, crushed, or grown shutLow, if the outlet is armoured and visible
Dry well or infiltration pitSoil percs and the water table stays below the pit bottom in springGround already saturated during the exact storm the pump is running forHigh on clay, low on sand
Storm sewer or approved connectionThe municipality allows it and you can get the permit and elevationSurcharged main backfeeds the line; connection isn't legal in that jurisdictionLow when permitted, severe when not

The dry well row is the one contractors underweight. A dry well sized off a dry-weather perc test will disappoint in April. If you're considering one, run the drawdown math the way you would for any infiltration decision — the same reasoning in catch basin vs dry well applies here, with one extra constraint: sump discharge arrives precisely when the surrounding soil is at its worst.

The honest fourth option is a surface swale to a lower point on the property, no structure at all. It's unglamorous, it doesn't freeze shut, and on the right lot it beats everything above.

What codes commonly require, and why you check before you quote

This varies more by jurisdiction than almost anything else in drainage work, so treat the following as a list of things to ask about, not a list of rules.

Common restrictions you'll run into:

  • No discharge to the sanitary sewer. This is close to universal in North America and often carries a fine. Older houses sometimes have an existing illegal tie-in you'll discover mid-job.
  • Storm sewer connection requires a permit and sometimes an inspection. Some municipalities allow it freely, some prohibit it, some allow it only with a backflow device.
  • Setback from the property line. Discharging onto a neighbour's lot is a civil problem whether or not it's a code problem, and the neighbour complaint arrives faster than any inspector.
  • No discharge onto sidewalks, driveways or public right-of-way, especially in freeze climates where it becomes an ice hazard and a liability question.
  • Seasonal surface-discharge rules. Some cold-climate municipalities require discharge above grade in summer and into a specific system in winter, or the reverse.

Make one phone call to the building department before you write the number. Ten minutes on the phone is cheaper than tearing out a connection after an inspection, and the answer often changes which termination you quote. Put the answer in your notes for that municipality — you'll bid there again.

Freeze protection is a design problem, not a product you add

The ice plug forms where the water stops moving and the pipe is shallow. That's almost always near the outlet, in the last six to ten feet, and it grows backward toward the house one cycle at a time.

Heat tape on a buried line is a bandage. It needs power at the far end, it fails silently, and the homeowner doesn't know it's dead until the basement is wet. It's not a design.

What actually works, in rough order of reliability:

  1. Continuous fall with no belly, and an outlet that can't submerge. Water that fully drains after every cycle has nothing to freeze. This is the whole answer when the lot allows it.
  2. Burial below frost, with the last few feet pitched hard to a free outlet. Frost depth varies enormously — a few inches in the mid-South, four feet or more across the northern tier and the Prairies. Use your local number, not a regional average.
  3. Oversized discharge pipe at the outlet end. Stepping up from 1.5" to 3" or 4" for the last stretch buys you a much larger cross-section to plug. A partial ice ring in a 4" pipe still passes water; in a 1.5" pipe it doesn't.
  4. A freeze-relief tee or above-grade bypass near the house. If the buried line plugs, water exits at a controlled point a few feet out instead of backing into the pit. Ugly, visible, and it keeps the basement dry.
  5. A visible, armoured outlet the homeowner can find in snow. A pop-up emitter that disappears under two feet of snow is a pop-up emitter nobody is going to clear.

Point four is the one that converts a February emergency into a June phone call. It costs very little at install. Explain it on the proposal so the homeowner understands the ugly pipe stub is deliberate and not sloppy work.

Price the call-back into the job, or the call-back prices itself

Say one in eight sump discharge jobs generates a winter call-back. Each one costs you two hours of a two-person crew plus a truck roll. Using a loaded crew rate — call it $120 an hour for two people plus truck in a lot of markets, with real spread — that's:

2 hours × $120 = $240 per call-back

$240 ÷ 8 jobs = $30 per job, carried across every sump job you sell

Thirty dollars a job is nothing against a proper outlet detail. But the number isn't really $30, because the call-back also costs you the slot in February when you had one crew and three emergencies, and it costs you the referral from a homeowner who now describes you as "they had to come back."

Substitute your own rate and your own call-back rate. If you don't know your call-back rate, that's the first number to start tracking — and to be straight with you, ScapesHQ doesn't do job costing or actuals, so that count lives in whatever you're already using. What it does hold is the permanent record of what you proposed and what the homeowner agreed to, which is the other half of the call-back conversation.

Because half of winter call-backs aren't failures at all. They're scope arguments. The homeowner thought the freeze-relief tee was a defect. They thought clearing the outlet was your job. They thought the dry well was rated for a hundred-year storm because nobody wrote down what it was actually sized for.

Write the discharge detail into the proposal with the same care you'd write the scope language on any drainage proposal: where it terminates, how deep it's buried, what maintenance the homeowner owns, and what conditions are outside the design. A paragraph on paper in October is worth two hours in the truck in February.

The site-visit checklist that prevents most of this

Everything above reduces to about eight minutes of work on the first visit, if you do it deliberately.

  • Shoot elevations from the pit rim to the proposed outlet. Write the fall down in inches, not "looks fine."
  • Walk the full discharge route and mark every low spot with a flag.
  • Open the pit and time one pump cycle. Note the pump's horsepower and the pit diameter.
  • Dig a shovel test at the proposed outlet. Wet clay at 18 inches tells you the dry well is out.
  • Photograph the property line and any hard surface within thirty feet of the outlet.
  • Call the building department before you price. Note the answer with the date.
  • Ask the homeowner one question: has the pump ever run in winter, and what happened?

That last question surfaces the history. A homeowner who says "it froze two years ago and my brother-in-law poured hot water on it" has just told you exactly what the previous installer got wrong and where.

Sump discharge sits at the boundary between the house and the yard, which is why it falls between the plumber's scope and the landscaper's scope and ends up designed by nobody. It's the same boundary you're already reading when you work through the five sources of water on a wet lot. Treat the discharge line as a drainage system in its own right — with a designed outfall, a designed failure mode and a written scope — and February stops being the month your schedule falls apart.

FAQ

How far from the foundation should a sump pump discharge?

Far enough to clear the disturbed backfill around the foundation and get onto undisturbed soil that isn't feeding the footing drain. On most residential lots that lands somewhere in the ten-to-fifteen foot range, further on tight clay and potentially less if you're terminating into a genuine daylight outfall on a slope. Measure your fall rather than picking a number off a chart.

Can a sump pump discharge into a French drain?

Sometimes, but be careful about direction of flow and elevation. If the French drain has a free daylight outfall below the sump discharge point, it can work. If the French drain is an infiltration trench sitting in saturated soil during the exact storm your pump is running for, you're putting the water back into the ground next to the house.

How do you keep a sump discharge line from freezing?

Design it so no water stands in the pipe after each cycle — continuous fall, no bellies, an outlet that can't submerge. Bury below your local frost depth where you can, step up to a larger pipe for the last stretch so a partial ice ring still passes water, and add an above-grade relief tee near the house so a plugged line vents outside rather than backing into the pit. Heat tape is a bandage, not a design.

Is it legal to connect a sump pump to the storm sewer?

It depends entirely on the municipality. Some allow it with a permit and a backflow device, some prohibit it outright, and almost all prohibit connection to the sanitary sewer. Call the building department before you write the price, because the answer often changes which termination you quote.

Who is responsible when a sump discharge line freezes, the contractor or the homeowner?

That depends on what your proposal said. If the scope documents where the line terminates, how it's buried, what the relief point is for, and what maintenance the homeowner owns — like clearing snow off the outlet — you have a conversation instead of an argument. If it says nothing, you're going out in February for free.

The short version

Sump discharge fails where water stands still: bellies in the line, outlets that submerge or get buried, and dry wells sized in dry weather. Get the water past the backfill zone onto undisturbed soil, keep continuous fall with no low spots, oversize the last few feet, and add an above-grade relief point near the house so a plugged line vents outside instead of into the pit. Then write all of that into the proposal — half of winter call-backs are scope arguments, not failures.
