French Drain vs Channel Drain: Which Water Are You Fixing?

A channel drain across a driveway will not dry out a soggy lawn, and a French drain under the lawn will not stop water sheeting into a garage.

September 13, 2026 · 10 min read

A contractor in Raleigh installed 60 feet of channel drain across the low end of a backyard patio. Clean cut, good slope, tied to daylight. The customer paid, the crew left, and three weeks later the same customer called because the lawn ten feet off the patio was still holding water after every rain.

Nothing was wrong with the install. The channel drain did exactly what channel drains do — it caught water running across a hard surface. The problem was that most of the water on that property was never running across the patio. It was already in the soil.

That's the whole distinction. Surface water moves on top of the ground and you intercept it where it travels. Subsurface water moves through the soil, and you have to give it somewhere to go before it reaches the surface at all. Pick the wrong one and the yard is still wet after the customer paid, and you own the callback.

The two systems do genuinely different jobs

A channel drain — trench drain, slot drain, whatever your supplier calls it — is a linear grate set flush with a hard surface. Water hits it, falls in, and runs out through a pipe. It's a surface interceptor. It only works on water that is already moving across something it can't soak into.

A French drain is a perforated pipe in a gravel envelope, wrapped in fabric, set below grade. Water in the surrounding soil moves into the gravel because gravel offers almost no resistance compared to clay, then into the pipe, then out. It's a subsurface collector. It doesn't care what's happening on the surface unless you deliberately feed it there.

The confusion comes from the fact that both end in a pipe going to daylight, and from a lot of homeowners using "French drain" as a generic word for "whatever fixes the wet part." Your estimate has to be more precise than the customer's vocabulary.

The diagnostic question that settles it in thirty seconds

Stand at the wet spot and ask one thing: where was this water one minute before it got here?

If the answer is "running down that driveway" or "coming off that patio" or "sheeting off the neighbor's concrete" — you have surface water, and you intercept it on the hard surface, upstream of the problem. That's a channel drain, or a swale, or regrading, depending on what's between you and the water.

If the answer is "it fell here and it's still here" or "it's coming up out of the ground at the toe of that slope" — you have subsurface water, and a grate on the surface does nothing, because there's no flow to intercept. That's a French drain.

Two field checks that tell you which answer is true:

  • Look at the wet spot 24 hours after rain stops. If it's still saturated and nothing is flowing into it, water is arriving from below or is simply trapped in place. Surface interception won't help.
  • Look for the sediment fan. Surface water leaves evidence — a silt line, a wash of fines, mulch pushed into a crescent, a scoured edge along the concrete. No fan, no meaningful surface flow.

A third check worth doing on any job over a few thousand dollars: dig a test hole 24 inches down at the wet spot and come back in an hour. If it holds water, you're dealing with soil that won't drain and possibly a water table. If it's dry at the bottom, the water is sitting in the top few inches and the fix is shallower than you thought. The failure mode for each one, spelled out

When a channel drain is the wrong call, it usually looks like this. The wet area is at a low point in the lawn, near a patio. The contractor cuts the patio edge, installs 40 feet of channel, ties it to daylight. After the next rain the channel runs for six minutes and then stops, and the lawn stays wet for two days. The channel caught the sliver of water that actually crossed the concrete, which was maybe five percent of the water causing the complaint.

When a French drain is the wrong call, it looks like this. Water sheets off a 900-square-foot driveway toward a garage. The contractor trenches a French drain along the garage apron, in gravel, under sod. For the first three storms it looks like it works, because gravel holds a lot of air space. Then the sediment carried in the sheet flow plugs the fabric and the top six inches of stone, and the whole thing becomes a speed bump. Sheet flow off concrete carries fines. Fabric plus fines equals a filter cake.

A French drain is a collector, not an inlet. The moment you ask it to swallow concentrated surface flow, you've given it a job it can't hold up to.

That's why the fix at a driveway edge is a channel drain, or a catch basin at the low point, or a properly shaped swale — something with an open throat and a sump you can clean out.

The jobs that genuinely need both, and how to sequence them

Plenty of properties have both problems. The mistake isn't installing both — it's installing them as one system.

Common combination job: a walk-out basement with a patio at the bottom of a slope. Surface water comes off the patio and off the hill above it. Subsurface water moves down through the hillside soil and emerges at the toe of the slope, which happens to be right where the patio meets the lawn.

Here's how that sequences:

  1. Intercept surface water first, upstream. Channel drain across the patio's low edge, or a swale cut across the slope above the patio. Take the concentrated flow out before it ever reaches the wet zone.
  2. Intercept subsurface water second, at the toe. French drain across the slope where the seep emerges, set deep enough to catch the flow path — usually below the wet layer, which you find by digging, not by guessing.
  3. Keep the collection separate until the last possible point. Two pipes, or one carrier pipe that the French drain ties into with a solid section, not a perforated one. If you run the channel drain's silt-laden flow through the perforated pipe, you've just seeded your French drain with sediment.
  4. One outfall, sized for the combined flow. The outlet has to carry both. A 4-inch solid line handling a French drain alone may back up when the channel drain contributes during a heavy storm. Size for the combined peak, and daylight it where the water actually gets away from the property.

That last point about separating the two before they combine is the one contractors skip, and it's the reason a combined system that worked great for two seasons stops working in year four.

How to price these differently, because they aren't the same job

Channel drain and French drain don't share a cost structure, and pricing them off the same per-foot number will burn you on half your jobs.

Channel drain cost concentrates in two places: the product and the hard-surface work. A run of commercial-grade polymer channel with a ductile grate costs several times what perforated pipe and stone cost per foot. Then you're saw-cutting, demoing, setting the channel in concrete, and patching back. Excavation volume is small; the finish work is not.

French drain cost concentrates in dirt, stone and restoration. You're moving a real volume of spoil, hauling in washed stone, and putting a lawn back together. Depth drives everything — the difference between 18 inches and 36 inches is roughly double the excavation volume and double the stone. The line-by-line breakdown is in how to price a French drain job.

Do the arithmetic yourself rather than trusting a rule of thumb. Take a 40-foot channel drain across a driveway:

  • Channel and grate: 40 ft × your supplier's current per-foot price, plus end caps and outlet fittings. Get a live quote — polymer channel pricing moves with resin costs and varies a lot by grate load rating.
  • Saw cutting: 80 linear feet of cut (both sides), at your blade-and-labor rate per foot.
  • Demo and haul: a 40 ft × 1.5 ft × 0.5 ft trench of concrete is 30 cubic feet, about 1.1 cubic yards. Concrete runs roughly two tons per cubic yard, so call it 2.2 tons to load and dump.
  • Concrete backfill: similar volume in, plus whatever you need under the channel to hold grade.
  • Outlet run: solid pipe to daylight at your standard per-foot trenched rate.
  • Loaded labor hours: your crew rate, which in most markets lands somewhere in the $35-$55 per loaded hour range per person once you count taxes, insurance, truck and non-billable time. Use your own figure — that range is wide for a reason.

Run the same structure for the French drain version and the two totals will not be close. On short runs in hard surface, the channel drain is often the more expensive job despite being shorter, which surprises customers who assume less digging means less money. Say it out loud in the walkthrough before they see the number.

If you've been quoting both off a single per-foot figure, the sanity check on per-foot pricing covers where the shortcut holds and where it falls apart.

What to put in the proposal so the callback doesn't happen

Most of the arguments that follow a drainage job aren't about workmanship. They're about scope the customer thought was included. If you install a channel drain at the driveway and the lawn stays soggy, the customer remembers "I paid a drainage guy and my yard is still wet."

Head that off in writing. Three lines, on the proposal, in the customer's language:

  • What water this system handles. "This system intercepts water running across the driveway before it reaches the garage." Specific, not generic.
  • What it does not handle. "This does not address saturated soil in the rear lawn, which is a separate condition." One sentence. It protects you and it builds trust.
  • The other condition, priced as an option. If you found a second problem, quote it. Don't bury it and don't give it away. A second option on the same proposal closes more often than a second visit does.

Optional scope like this is easier to present as a clean choice than as a paragraph of hedging. Options on a proposal, each with its own price and its own one-line description of what it fixes, is one of the things ScapesHQ handles — and it's the difference between a customer who declines the second system knowingly and a customer who never heard about it. The through-line here is small. Figure out where the water was one minute ago. Install for that. Write down what you didn't fix. Most wet-yard callbacks die right there.

FAQ

Can a French drain handle water coming off a driveway?

Not well, and not for long. Sheet flow off a hard surface carries fine sediment that plugs the filter fabric and the top of the stone envelope, usually within a few seasons. Use a channel drain, a catch basin or a shaped swale for concentrated surface flow, and keep that flow in solid pipe until after it passes your French drain.

How deep does a French drain need to be?

Deep enough to sit below the wet layer you're intercepting, which you find by digging a test hole rather than by applying a standard. Many residential runs land between 18 and 36 inches, but a perimeter drain at a foundation or a seep at the toe of a slope can be considerably deeper. Depth drives excavation volume and stone quantity, so confirm it before you price the job.

Is a channel drain more expensive than a French drain?

Per foot, often yes. The channel product with a load-rated grate costs several times what perforated pipe and washed stone cost, and then you add saw cutting, concrete demo, haul-off and patch-back. French drain cost sits in excavation volume, stone and lawn restoration. Price both from your own line items rather than assuming the one with less digging is cheaper.

What if the yard has both surface and subsurface water?

Install both, but as two systems that stay separate until the last possible point. Intercept surface flow upstream with a channel drain or swale, catch the subsurface flow with a French drain where it emerges, and tie them together only in solid pipe near the outfall. Size the outlet for the combined peak flow, not for either system alone.

How do I tell a homeowner the fix won't dry out the whole yard?

Put it in the proposal in one sentence, in their words: what this system catches, and what condition it does not address. Then quote the second condition as a separate option with its own price. Customers accept a limit they were told about up front, and they resent one they discover after paying.

The short version

Stand at the wet spot and ask where that water was one minute earlier. If it was running across a hard surface, you need a channel drain or catch basin set to intercept it upstream. If it fell there and stayed, or it's seeping out of a slope, you need a French drain below the wet layer. Properties with both problems need both systems kept separate until they join in solid pipe near the outfall, and the proposal needs one line saying what you did not fix.
